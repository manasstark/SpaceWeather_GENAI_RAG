Abstract
In 2024 May, the scienti ﬁc community observed intense solar eruptions that resulted in a great geomagnetic storm
and auroral extensions, highlighting the need to document and quantify these events. This study mainly focuses on
their quanti ﬁcation. The source active region (AR; NOAA Active Region 13664 ) evolved from 113 to 2761
millionths of the solar hemisphere between May 4 and 14. NOAA AR 13664 ’s magnetic free energy surpassed
1033 erg on May 7, triggering 12 X-class ﬂares on May 8 –15. Multiple interplanetary coronal mass ejections
(ICMEs) were produced from this AR, accelerating solar energetic particles toward Earth. According to satellite
and interplanetary scintillation data, at least four ICMEs erupted from AR 13664, eventually overcoming and
combining each other. The shock arrival at 17:05 UT on May 10 signi ﬁcantly compressed the magnetosphere
down to ≈5.04 RE and triggered a deep Forbush Decrease. GOES satellite data and ground-based neutron monitors
conﬁrmed a ground-level enhancement from 2 UT to 10 UT on 2024 May 11. The ICMEs induced exceptional
geomagnetic storms, peaking at a provisional Dst index of −412 nT at 2 UT on May 11, marking the sixth-largest
storm since 1957. The AE and AL indices showed great auroral extensions that located the AE /AL stations into
the polar cap. We gathered auroral records at that time and reconstructed the equatorward boundary of the visual
auroral oval to 29 °. 8 invariant latitude. We compared naked-eye and camera auroral visibility, providing critical
caveats on their difference. We also con ﬁrmed global disturbances of the storm-enhanced density of the
ionosphere.

1. Introduction
Sunspot active regions occasionally direct interplanetary
coronal mass ejections (ICMEs) to the Earth, disrupting near-
Earth space environments, altering cosmic ray radiation
levels, perturbing terrestrial magnetic ﬁelds, and extending the
auroral oval equatorward (T. Pulkkinen 2007; M. Temmer
2022; E. W. Cliver et al. 2022;K .K u s a n o2023;I .G .U s o s k i n
2023). Empirical evidence suggests that such solar eruptions
occur more often in larger solar cycles, especially in their
maximum and declining phase, although the maximum
magnitude of the solar eruption is not necessarily dependent
on the amplitude of the host solar cycle (E. K. J. Kilpua et al.
2015; L. Lefèvre et al. 2016; S. C. Chapman et al. 2020;
M. J. Owens et al. 2022; S. C. Chapman & T. Dudok de
Wit 2024). Such solar eruptions occasionally cause signi ﬁcant
terrestrial impacts, such as great geomagnetic storms and
increases of charged particle ﬂux throughout the atmospheric
proﬁle, leading to an increased count rate of ground-based
particle radiation detectors. These increases are known as
ground-level enhancements (GLEs; E. W. Cliver et al. 2022;
I. Usoskin et al. 2023). Extending analyses to the extremity of
solar eruptions and their terrestrial consequences is essential to
bridge modern scienti ﬁc data with past supersized events,
develop statistical analyses, and understand impacts on terrestrial
infrastructure (I. A. Daglis 2001; D. N. Baker et al. 2008;

In this context, the last solar cycle, Solar Cycle 24 (from
2008 December to 2019 December ), was notably quiet in terms
of disturbances in the solar –terrestrial environments (S. Watari
2017; V. Manu et al. 2022). This solar cycle marked the lowest
amplitude in sunspot number in the the 20th to 21st centuries
since Solar Cycle 14 in 1902 January –1913 July (F. Clette et al.
2023). Solar Cycle 24 witnessed up to X14.8 in 2017
September in the revised scale (Table 7 of H. Hudson et al.
2024), geomagnetic storms reached a minimum Dst = −234 nT
in 2015 March (N. Gopalswamy et al. 2015, 2024;Y .D .L i u
et al. 2015; World Data Centre for Geomagnetism at Kyoto
et al. 2015a; M. Piersanti et al. 2017; D. Webb & N. Nitta
2017), and hosted only two GLEs in 2012 May and 2017
September (A. L. Mishev et al. 2014, 2018; I. Usoskin et al.
2020).
The current solar cycle, Solar Cycle 25, commenced in 2019
December. Predictions suggest that this cycle may be either as
quiet as or slightly more active than Solar Cycle 24 (P. Bhowmik
&D .N a n d y 2018; W. D. Pesnell & K. H. Schatten 2018;
S. W. McIntosh et al. 2020;K .M u r s u l a2023; L. A. Upton &
D. H. Hathaway 2023; F. Clette et al. 2024), whereas lessons
from the previous cycle caution us to closely monitor how these
forecasts align with the actual temporal evolution of the sunspot
number (W. D. Pesnell 2016, 2020). Despite these predictions,
Solar Cycle 25 has shown increased activity compared to Solar
Cycle 24 in terms of disturbances in terrestrial environments
(D. Oliveira et al. 2024). Even prior to reaching its maximum,
Solar Cycle 25 has already witnessed one severe geomagnetic
storm (with a minimum Dst below −200 nT according to the
classic classiﬁcation of C. A. Loewe & G. W. Prölss 1997) in
2023 April, reaching a minimum Dst of −213 nT (L. Li &
S. Jin 2024), and one GLE in 2021 October (A. L. Mishev et al.
2022; A. Papaioannou et al. 2022; K.-L. Klein et al. 2022).
In 2024 May, Solar Cycle 25 witnessed the emergence of a
signiﬁcant sunspot active region, triggering multiple solar
eruptions and leading to a GLE and a substantial geomagnetic
storm. Notably, this geomagnetic disturbance reached −412 nT
in the provisional Dst index, which is classi ﬁed as a “great”
storm (min Dst < −350 nT) according to C. A. Loewe &
G. W. Prölss (1997) and ranked as the sixth greatest in the Dst
index starting from 1957 and the ninth greatest in the Dxt index
starting from 1932 (A. Karinen & K. Mursula 2005; WDC for
Geomagnetism at Kyoto et al. 2015a; K. Mursula et al. 2022).
This geomagnetic storm extended auroral displays to lower
magnetic latitudes (e.g., S. Ghosh 2024; M. Greshko 2024;
D. K. Karan et al. 2024). This solar and geomagnetic storm has
attracted the scienti ﬁc community to start analyses on several
aspects such as their solar radio bursts (O. Kruparova et al.
2024), geomagnetic impacts in the Kp index and HPO index
(Y. Yamazaki et al. 2024), local geomagnetic and ionospheric
impacts around Italy (L. Spogli et al. 2024), auroral imageries,
and resultant satellite drags (W. E. Parker & R. Linares 2024).
Obviously there are more to come.
This solar –terrestrial storm serves as a valuable reference
point for connecting modern scienti ﬁc data with past great
storms, particularly regarding geomagnetic impacts. Therefore,
it is valuable to have a ﬂash global overview of this solar and
geomagnetic storm on the basis of what is available in the
immediate aftermath. Here, we quantify observational data sets
from this solar–terrestrial storm to facilitate further analysis and
discussions regarding this exceptional event and to contextua-
lize it with historical great storms.
2. The Source Solar Eruptions
This event occurred in an ascending phase —or near the
maximum—of SC25. The daily sunspot number (F. Clette et al.
2023) signiﬁcantly increased on May 2, reaching 187 on May 6
and 186 on May 12. At the time of writing, some predictions
place the expected maximum from late 2024 to early 2025
(S. W. McIntosh et al. 2020; L. A. Upton & D. H. Hathaway
2023; F. Clette et al. 2024).
A new active region (AR), known as NOAA Active Region
13664 according to the NOAA /SWPC region number, came
into the southeastern limb of the visible side of the solar disk on
May 1. Its temporal and spatial evolutions were documented in
sunspot drawings and images in several observatories, such as
the Royal Observatory of Belgium using the Uccle Solar
Equatorial table (USET) telescopes (S. Bechet & F. Clette
2022). This sunspot group started as a bipolar group, with two
penumbrae and some intermediate sunspots in between. This
active region developed rapidly from May 3 –7 and crossed the
central meridian on May 7. The area measured from the USET
sunspot drawing with the Digi Sun software (e.g., F. Clette
2011; H. Hayakawa et al. 2023a) was 1312 millionths of the
visible solar hemisphere (hereafter MSH ). This group was still
growing and developed into a Fkc McIntosh type, consisting of
several moderately sized penumbral sunspots. This group
continued to grow until May 9 up to an area of 2761 MSH,
consisting of a compact penumbra containing most of the spots,
as shown in the sunspot drawing and white-light image
(Figure 1). The area remained relatively stable for a period
before rotating over the limb on May 14. The evolution of the
group area, as measured from sunspot drawings, is illustrated in
Figure 2, showing a rapid increase until May 10.
The unsigned magnetic ﬂux constantly increased from May
7 to 1.9 × 1023 Mx before it reached the west limb. We
extrapolated the nonlinear force-free ﬁeld (NLFFF; S. Inoue
et al. 2014) of this AR using near-real-time data of the
photospheric vector magnetic ﬁeld from the Spaceweather HMI
Active Region Patch (SHARP) near-real-time (NRT) data set
(M. G. Bobra et al. 2014). Figure 2 shows the magnetic free
energy, which is de ﬁned as the difference between the volume-
integrated magnetic energy of the NLFFF and the potential
magnetic ﬁeld. We can see that the magnetic free energy
increased before the M-class ﬂares on May 2 and quickly grew
on May 7 to ≈1033 erg before the consecutive X-class ﬂares
(Figure 2 and Table 1). The maximum values of the unsigned
magnetic ﬂux and magnetic free energy are as large as those of
AR NOAA 12192, which provides the largest sunspot area,
unsigned ﬂux, and magnetic free energy in SC 24 (K. Karimov
et al. 2024).

magnetic ﬁelds from the SHARP-NRT data and the potential

The notable feature of this region is the presence of several
high free energy regions (HiFERs) where the nonpotential ﬁeld
intensity exceeds 1000 G. Likely, the large free energy and the

consecutive large ﬂares (K. Kusano et al. 2020).
Eventually, this active region hosted 12 X-class ﬂares from
May 1 to 15 (Table 1 and Figure 2). Most of these were
produced during transit over the visible solar hemisphere, while
four were produced after the AR went over the western limb
onto the far side. This AR produced a ﬂare as great as X8.7.
Even though the ﬂare partially occurred on the western limb,
this was the largest solar ﬂare in the current solar cycle (SC25)
at the time of writing at that time. Obviously there is more to
come in this solar cycle, as exempli ﬁed by the X9.0 ﬂare from
NOAA AR 13842 on October 3. Figure 2 shows the soft X-ray
ﬂux continuously above the M1 level from May 8 to 12.
3. Coronal Mass Ejections
These solar eruptions of 2024 May released a considerable
number of CMEs. From 2024 May 5 to 15, the Large Angle
Spectroscopic Coronagraph (LASCO) observed at least 19
major CMEs (width 60°), including 10 halo CMEs, which
were produced by NOAA AR 13664 according to the data set
being prepared by Gopalswamy ’s team. The calibrated LASCO
data became available in 2024 August in the Coordinated Data
Analysis Workshop (CDAW) for creating the CME Catalog
(S. Yashiro et al. 2004, N. Gopalswamy 2009).
We have identiﬁed the CME that was most likely responsible
for inducing the space environment disturbances observed near
the Earth on May 11 with one (X1.0 at 22:36 UT ) on May 8
that occurred in quick succession. The Solar Dynamics
Observatory/Atmospheric Imaging Assembly (SDO/AIA;
J. R. Lemen et al. 2012) observed the eruptive features in
both ﬂares. However, only a single halo CME was observed at
22:36 UT (#2 in Table 1, hereafter referred to as CME2 ),a s
shown in Figure 4. The two eruptions observed in the AIA
likely merged before reaching the ﬁeld of view of LASCO C2.
The projected speed of CME2 was 952 km s −1 and if it traveled
at this speed without any deceleration /acceleration, it would
have reached Earth at ≈17:33 UT on May 10 (Table 1). While
this is slightly later than the actual CME arrival of 17:05 UT on
May 10, this offset shows a considerably good agreement in
contrast with the usual error margins (up to ±8t o ±12 hr) of
the CME arrival time predictions (N. Gopalswamy et al.
2001, 2005, Y. Zhong et al. 2024). In several cases, CMEs
propagate earthward faster than expected from their apparent
propagation speed measured in coronagraphs. In such cases,
the actual arrival time comes earlier than the estimated arrival
time. Based on these ﬁndings, we believe that CME2 was
associated with the sudden storm commencement (SSC) at
17:05 UT on May 10. This estimated arrival time indicated that
CME2 likely stacked on CME1 (#1 in Table 1). Additionally,
two more halo CMEs were observed, which were associated
with M-class ﬂares (Table 2) produced by NOAA AR 13664.
CME2 likely collided and stacked upon the three preceding
CMEs originating from the same AR, resulting in a complex
CME structure, as discussed later. Therefore, the great
geomagnetic storm of 2024 May 10 –11 is not associated with
a single fast CME but with a complex accumulation of multiple
CMEs and southward interplanetary magnetic ﬁelds (IMFs).
Subsequently, the AR produced more halo CMEs upon
those, including two major ﬂares (X1.1 at 09:24 on May 9 and
X3.8 at 07:12 on May 10 ) launched CMEs at similar speeds
(1226 and 1006 km s −1) and another (X5.8 at 01:36 on May
11) launched a faster CME (1512 km s−1), as shown in
Figure 4. These CMEs probably arrived on Earth at ≈9:00
UT on May 12. This small difference between the observed and
estimated arrival times, as indicated in Table 1, may imply that
the preceding CME sequence cleared the interplanetary space,

CMEs are typically faster or slower than their measured
projected velocity, owing to the propagation process in the
interplanetary space and their actual 3D structure. This is
because the measured projected velocity, which is based on
transverse expansion speed, is not necessarily equal to the
propagation speed in the Sun –Earth direction. The difference in
the speeds depends on the shape of the CME. Therefore, the
actual arrival time mostly varies from the estimated arrival
time, as indicated in Table 1. Furthermore, NOAA AR 13664
produced multiple CMEs at signi ﬁcantly different speeds over
several days. Therefore, the relationship between the CMEs
observed through coronagraphs and the CME structures
observed in situ can be considerably more complex. To
interpret this relationship, magnetohydrodynamic (MHD)
simulations of multiple CME propagations should be further
analyzed. Our results are considerably different from Spogli
et al. (2024), who used the CME speeds automatically obtained
from CACTus (E. Robbrecht & D. Berghmans 2004; E. Rob-
brecht et al. 2009) and associated the solar eruptions on May
9–11 with this great geomagnetic storm. The CACTus median
speeds are generally lower than manual measurements, and
their maximum speeds are occasionally extremely high (e.g.,
Figure 14 of E. Robbrecht and D. Berghmans 2004). Owing to
their complexity, in-depth analyses of the interplay between
CMEs are currently underway by Daikou Shiota ’s team.

During the period under investigation in this study, speciﬁcally
on 2024 May 10 –11, we observed a signi ﬁcant variation in
cosmic ray activity. Corresponding to the arrival of CMEs at
17:05 UT, as shown by the SSC in the Kakioka Observatory
(Kakioka Magnetic Observatory et al. 2013; see details in
Section 6),as i g n iﬁcant Forbush Decrease(S. E. Forbush 1937) in
cosmic ray intensities was observed in all available data sets with

monitor count rate of magnitude 15% or more was reached

Around 2 UT on May 11, the ﬂux of high-energy(>500 MeV)
protons started to increase, as registered by the GOES-16 satellite
(Figure 6). Notable variation of the solar proton ﬂux triggered
GLE alerts, e.g., an alert of the Bartol group, the Athens GLE alert
++ by the Athens group, and an alert issued at 01:55 UT by the
Kazakhstan group (private communication with O. Kraykunova).
However, the two latter alerts were produced using several
midlatitude stations and are likely due to a reduction in the cutoff

(WDC for Geomagnetism at Kyoto et al. 2015b) initiated
signiﬁcant development at 17:06 UT on May 10 and increased
to 2379 nT and −1830 nT, respectively, at 17:28 UT. These
peak values were reached in 22 minutes. The abrupt jump in
the provisional AE index was probably regarded as a shock-
triggered substorm (S.-I. Akasofu & J. K. Chao 1980;
B. T. Tsurutani & X.-Y. Zhou 2003). The ionospheric current
associated with the main impulse of the SSC have also
contributed to the abrupt jumps in these indices (T. Araki 1994;
T. Zhang et al. 2023). During the storm ’s main phase, the
provisional AE and AL indices reached peak values of 4098
and −3797 nT, respectively, at 19:48 UT on May 10.
Subsequently, these indices remained within < |2000| nT
during the main storm phase. During the storm recovery phase,
these indices reached peak values of 3982 and −3797 nT at
09:00 UT on May 11. According to a statistical study by
M. Nakamura et al. (2015), up to 16 events occurred during
which AL exceeded −3000 nT from 1996 to 2012, and the
recurrence period for AL of −3797 nT was 1.49 yr. In this
sense, the provisional AL index of −3797 nT was not that
extreme. We have to note that the AE and AL indices are not
necessarily proportional to the intensity of the auroral
electrojet.

the magnetospheric convection (P. C. Brandt et al. 2002;
Y. Ebihara et al. 2002; K. K. Hashimoto et al. 2002).

reconstruction of the equatorward boundaries of the auroral
oval down to 35 °. 0 ILAT and 36 °. 7 ILAT, respectively. In the
Northern Hemisphere, a Mexican report from Chirimoyos
(N23°26′, W105 °48′,3 1 °. 0 MLAT) allowed us to reconstruct
the equatorward boundaries of the auroral oval down to 35 °.5
ILAT based on the reported naked-eye auroral altitude of 60 °.
This is slightly more equatorward than what we can reconstruct
from another Mexican report from El Peyote (N22°41′, W100°
36′,3 0 °. 7 ILAT) with a reported naked-eye auroral altitude of
25°. Overall, our reconstructions in both hemispheres were
reasonably consistent: 29 °. 8 ILAT in the Southern Hemisphere
and 35 °. 5 ILAT in the Northern Hemisphere.
Caveats must be noted on these estimates here. First, their
naked-eye visibility is considerably different from the camera
visibility (Appendix A). Second, SAR arcs often reach farther
toward the magnetic equator from the auroral ovals. It is
certainly tempting to associate this considerable visibility
stretch with the SAR arcs (L. Spogli et al. 2024), whereas this
association con ﬂicts with the analysis of the GOLD imagers
(D. K. Karan et al. 2024).
The SAR arcs appear monochromatically in reddish
coloration, in contrast to the actual reddish auroral emissions
(J. U. Kozyra et al. 1997; K. Shiokawa et al. 1997).
M. Mendillo et al. (2016) reported that patches of emission
at the oxygen green line (557.7 nm) are superimposed to the
SAR arcs. After considering the possibility that precipitating
ions gave rise to the oxygen red line, M. H. Rees and
C. S. Deehr (1961) suggested that precipitation of low energy
electrons could result in the emission at the oxygen green line,
together with head conduction from the plasmapause –ring
current interaction region, which could result in the SAR arcs.
For now, we cannot exclude the possibility that the
monochromatic reddish aurora witnessed at low latitudes was
associated with the SAR arcs, as was also the case with
historical auroral reports with monochromatic reddish
coloration.
Therefore, we need to choose records with nonreddish colors
or explicit structures (Appendix B) to rule out SAR arcs and
conservatively reconstruct the equatorward boundaries of the
auroral oval to be free from contamination of SAR arcs. The
reports that we used to reconstruct the equatorward boundaries
of the auroral oval in both hemispheres included nonreddish
colorations (pinkish and purplish components in Brom ﬁeld in
Australia, and pinkish components in Chirimoyos in Mexico;
see Appendix B) and/or explicit structures (in Chirimoyos in
Mexico and Tivoli Astrofarm in Namibia ), as partially seen in
Figure 14. Our reconstructions of the equatorward boundary of
the auroral oval support analyses of the GOLD imager that
suggested auroral stretch down to the southern tip of South
Africa and South America (D. K. Karan et al. 2024), rather than
the SAR arc hypothesis based on a model calculation
(L. Spogli et al. 2024). Therefore, our reconstructions enhance
the reliability of an extension of the equatorward boundary of
the auroral oval during the 2024 May storm in comparison to
the modeling results of L. Spogli et al. (2024).

9. Summary and Discussion
This paper presented a ﬂash report that reviewed the data from
the 2024 May solar and geomagnetic storms. The area of the
source active region, NOAA AR 13664, rapidly increased from
113 to 2761 MSH from May 1 to 9 (Figures 1 and 2).T h i sr a p i d
growth led to an increase in the total energy of the nonlinear
force-free ﬁeld, potential magnetic ﬁeld, and free magnetic
energy, especially from May 7 onward, producing 12 X-class
ﬂares up to X8.7 between May 8 and 15 (Table 1 and Figure 2).
This AR was associated with at least 10 halo CMEs. By
evaluating their velocities, we could associate the CME launched
at 22:36 UT on 2024 May 8, with the great geomagnetic storm
that originated from the SSC at 17:05 UT on May 10 (Figure 4).
Thus, the propagation time of the CME was 42 hr 29 minutes.

accumulate background solar wind and might support CME –

One of these solar eruptions triggered a signi ﬁcant Forbush
Decrease in cosmic ray intens ity and a solar proton event. The
CMEs caused a relatively large Forbush Decrease with the
magnitude of 15%, as seen in neutron monitors ’ count rate.
Subsequently, both the polar neutron monitors and the GOES-16
satellite registered a notable cosmic ray increase from≈2:00–3:00

These CME arrivals considerably compressed the magneto-
sphere. THEMIS measurements conﬁrmed their crossings over the
boundary of the magnetosphere at ≈8.24 RE at 17:05 UT
(Figure 7), ≈8.51 RE at 17:05 UT (Figure 8), ≈5.04 RE at 19:12

the observations of THEMIS-E and THEMIS-A during their
crossings at 19:12 and 19:19 UT, respectively, indicated that the
magnetopause underwent a signiﬁcant inward motion beyond the
geosynchronous orbit(6.6 RE). Additionally, major loss processes
for radiation belt particles occur during such great events
(D. L. Turner et al. 2012). Moreover, these events have profound
space weather implications for geospace satellites because they can
be exposed to energetic radiation belt particles that may partially or
completely damage their electronic systems(A. D. P. Hands et al.
2018). For example, if a satellite reenters the magnetosphere and
radiation belts when the magnetopause moves back out, the
likelihood of damage is ampli ﬁed if energetic particle injections
associated with rapid energetic particle injections and wave –
particle interactions increase(D. N. Baker et al. 2017; H. C. Koons
&J .F .F e n n e l l2006). Therefore, satellitesin geospace may have
been exposed to harsh space weather conditions during the
geomagnetic storm of 2024 May 10.
The SSC at 17:05 UT upon shock arrival was observed at
multiple observatories, such as Kakioka (≈78 nT) and San
Fernando (≈130 nT), indicating local enhancements in the
duskside, especially in the summer hemisphere. This LT effect
probably enhanced SSCs in the European sector.
The resultant geomagnetic storm developed to minimum
provisional Dst and Dxt index values of −412 and −415.5 nT,
respectively, at 2:00 UT on May 11. In the provisional AE and
AL indices, the auroral electrojet was intensi ﬁed up to 4098
and −3797 nT during the storm main phase, respectively, and
3982 and −3797 nT during the storm recovery phase,
respectively. However, the intensity in both indices reduced
between the phases, probably because the auroral oval moved
equatorward and remained away from the AE /AL observa-
tories. However, their magnitudes should not be exaggerated as
statistical studies have indicated that the return period of the
Dst index is 5.7 yr (K. Tsubouchi & Y. Omura 2007) and that
of the AE index 1.49 yr (M. Nakamura et al. 2015).I ti s
possible that geomagnetic activity during Solar Cycle 24 was
considerably lower than that during normal solar cycles.
Furthermore, the DP2 ionospheric current was signi ﬁcantly
enhanced (Figure 14). This enhancement manifested as an
intensiﬁcation of the magnetospheric convection, which is highly
likely to cause earthward penetration of the hot plasma originating
in the plasma sheet, thereby enhancing the ring current.
This storm also induced considerable disturbances in the
ionosphere (Figure 16), leading to an SED in the ionosphere,
large rTECs in the lower MLATs (20°–50°) on the duskside
(13–20 MLT), and tongues of the ionosphere extending from the
dayside to the nightside in the northern polar region, despite
unfavorable seasonality. Theseﬁndings indicated the occurrence of
an intense convective electric ﬁeld in the high-to-low MLATs in
the ionosphere. These TEC records also con ﬁrmed rTEC
enhancements in a narrow MLAT band of 45 °–55° on the
nightside, conﬁrming the equatorward extension of the auroral oval
in accordance with the visual records. The rTEC measurements
also revealed a plasmapause in the inner magnetosphere,
characterized by a depletion in the 25 °–45° MLAT region and a
considerably negative storm globally during the recovery phase.

age (1957 onward), the min Dst of −412 nT is comparable to

modern naked-eye observations of auroral ovals during geomag-
netic storms and comparing them with those of historical ones is
crucial for better contextualizing great storms.

Immediately after this storm, auroral photographs were
uploaded from various regions worldwide. This signi ﬁcant
geographical extent can be partially explained by the differences
in the sensitivities of naked-eye observers and cameras. Today,
we have access to signiﬁcantly better optical instruments, such as
cameras, than during past great space weather events. Moreover,
space weather forecasts have allowed and motivated people to
observe aurorae at the right time and direct cameras to the sky to
ﬁgure out whether they can capture aurorae, as con ﬁrmed by
several case reports. Furthermore, Internet /SNS accessibility
allows observers to swiftly exchange reports /photos, which
motivates more people to search for visible aurorae. However,
light pollution in urban areas hinders aurora visibility in the night
sky (M. Smith 2009; F. Falchi et al. 2016). Therefore, witnesses
of this auroral storm have three advantages and one disadvantage
when documenting auroral extensions during great geomagnetic
storms compared to those witnessed in historical auroral storms.
This study is no more than a ﬂash report conducted using
provisional data sets, and more precise data sets must be
accumulated and compared with the modeling results. Through
further analyses, these solar and geomagnetic storm features
may allow us to bridge our modern scienti ﬁc knowledge with
that of historical great solar and geomagnetic storms and better
assess space weather extremes.
